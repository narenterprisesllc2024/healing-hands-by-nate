Five Bullet Friday · The Long Weekend Edition

July 3, 2026

It's the Fourth. Or almost. The smoker's heating up, the lawn chairs are out, and the next three days are the kind of weekend Missouri summers were made for. Five things before you unplug.

1. A move — the half-kneeling hip flexor stretch

Lawn chairs, car seats, boat seats, bleachers at the fireworks — everything this weekend puts your hips in the same position: flexed, shortened, stuck. By Sunday night, your hip flexors will be tight enough to pull your pelvis forward and put your low back in a bind.

Get ahead of it. Drop one knee to the ground (on a towel or grass), other foot flat in front of you, knee at 90 degrees. Tuck your tailbone slightly — think “belt buckle up” — and shift your weight forward until you feel a stretch in the front of the hip on the kneeling side. Hold 45 seconds. Switch sides. Keep your torso upright — if you arch your back to go deeper, you're cheating the stretch and loading the spine. Twice a day through the weekend.

2. A tool — a tennis ball and a wall ($3)

You can get a can of three at any sporting goods store or dollar store. You only need one.

Stand with your back to a wall, place the tennis ball between you and the wall, right next to your spine at mid-back height. Lean in with moderate pressure. Roll slowly up and down by bending and straightening your knees. When you find a knot — stop. Lean in. Breathe. Ten seconds, then move on.

This covers the rhomboids and mid-traps — the muscles that seize up from carrying coolers, holding toddlers, and standing at the grill for two hours with your arms forward. A tennis ball has more give than a lacrosse ball, which makes it better here — firm enough to work, soft enough not to bruise. Two minutes per side.

3. A nugget — a warm shower before bed does more than you think

A 2019 meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews (Haghayegh et al., University of Texas) looked at over 5,000 studies on passive body heating and sleep. The finding: a warm shower or bath one to two hours before bed significantly reduced the time it took to fall asleep and improved overall sleep quality. The mechanism is counterintuitive — warm water brings blood to the surface, and when you get out, your core temperature drops. That temperature drop is the signal your brain uses to initiate sleep.

The timing matters. Too close to bed and you're still warm when you lie down. One to two hours out gives the body time to cool. If you're running the holiday weekend hard — long days, late nights, heat exposure — a warm shower around 9 PM might be the easiest thing you do for your sleep. Pairs well with the pre-bed stretch routine I wrote about this week (link below).

4. A practice — two minutes barefoot on grass

Step outside barefoot. Stand on the grass. Feel the ground — temperature, texture, the slight unevenness under your feet. Stand still for a minute, then walk slowly, paying attention to how your feet respond to the surface. Toes spread, arch adjusts, the small stabilizer muscles in your foot and ankle activate in ways they never do inside a shoe on a flat floor.

This isn't a health trend pitch. It's a proprioception reset. Your feet have more nerve endings per square inch than almost any other part of your body, and they spend all day in shoes getting zero input. Two minutes of real ground gives your nervous system actual sensory data instead of the nothing it's used to. Do it with your morning coffee this weekend.

5. A seasonal note

Fourth of July in Missouri. The kind of weekend where you do everything — grill, swim, watch fireworks from a blanket, carry kids on your shoulders, sit in a lawn chair for six hours straight. It's all good.

But Monday comes. And the weekend usually leaves a receipt — tight hips, a stiff upper back, a neck that won't turn right. Take care of the first two while you're out there. The hip stretch from bullet one, the tennis ball from bullet two. Two minutes each.

When the weekend's over, the table's open Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. A post-holiday session is one of the best times to come in — everything's fresh, everything's findable, and one hour clears what would otherwise harden into a month-long pattern.
