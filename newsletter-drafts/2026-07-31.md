# Five Bullet Friday — The Dog Days Edition

*July 31*

August starts tomorrow. Missouri has been running 95 and humid for weeks, the garden hose is warm before you even turn it on, and your body has been keeping score since Memorial Day. Five things before the last month of summer hits.

---

### 1. A move — the standing upper trap release

Your upper traps are the muscles between your neck and your shoulders, and right now they're probably hiked up around your ears. Heat, driving, stress, mowing, carrying anything — they all load the same spot. This one is simple and you can do it standing anywhere.

Drop your right ear toward your right shoulder. Reach your right hand over the top of your head and rest it gently on the left side — no pulling, just the weight of your hand. Let gravity and the hand do the work for 30 seconds. You should feel the stretch run from behind your ear down into the top of your left shoulder. Switch sides.

If you want more, try it seated with your opposite hand gripping the edge of the chair or tucked under your thigh. That anchors the shoulder down and deepens the stretch without forcing it. Sixty seconds, both sides. Do it every time you catch yourself with your shoulders in your ears.

### 2. A tool — a reusable gel ice pack ($5-8)

The tennis ball, the foam roller, and the golf ball are for releasing. This one is for calming things down after the releasing is done — or when the heat itself is the problem.

A basic gel ice pack, the kind that costs six bucks at any pharmacy, is the most underused recovery tool in summer. Wrap it in a thin towel. Lay it across the back of your neck for 10 minutes at the end of the day. The back of your neck is a thermoregulation zone — cooling it lowers your perceived body temperature faster than anything except a cold shower, and it relaxes the upper traps and suboccipitals at the same time.

You can also drape it across your low back after yard work or tuck it behind your mid-back in a chair. Keep two in the freezer so one's always ready. Skip the fancy ones with straps — a flat gel pack and a dish towel work better than anything I've seen at twice the price.

### 3. A nugget — dehydration makes your muscles hurt more

You know you're supposed to drink water. Here's a reason beyond the obvious.

Ogino et al. published a study in *Experimental Brain Research* (2014) showing that even mild dehydration — losing just 1-2% of your body weight in water — measurably increased pain sensitivity in their subjects. The threshold for discomfort dropped. Things that shouldn't hurt, hurt. Things that already hurt, hurt more.

Your fascia — the connective tissue wrapping every muscle fiber — needs water to stay pliable. When it dries out, it stiffens. Tight fascia doesn't stretch well, doesn't slide well, and doesn't release under pressure the way hydrated tissue does. This is why the same knot that loosens in one session during spring can take two sessions in August. The tissue itself is less cooperative when you're running dry.

In this heat, "I drink enough water" usually isn't enough. If you're outside for any length of time, you need more than you think — and by the time you feel thirsty, you're already behind.

### 4. A practice — legs up the wall (2 minutes)

Find a wall. Lie on your back with your hips close to the base. Walk your legs up until they're resting against the wall, roughly straight, heels toward the ceiling. Let your arms rest at your sides, palms up. Close your eyes. Stay for two minutes.

That's it. No technique, no counting, no breathwork required — though slow breathing makes it better.

This inverts the fluid that's been pooling in your legs and feet all day in the heat. It takes pressure off the low back. And it triggers the same parasympathetic downshift as the humming practice from a few weeks ago — your nervous system reads "lying down, legs elevated, eyes closed" as a clear signal to stop running the daytime program.

If your hamstrings are tight, scoot your hips a few inches away from the wall. Comfort matters more than perfect form. Best done in the evening, after you've been on your feet. It pairs well with the ice pack on your neck from bullet two.

### 5. A seasonal note

End of July. The dog days. Everything you've done since May is in your body right now — the mowing, the driving, the projects, the heat. August hasn't even started and most people I see on the table are carrying a full summer's worth of accumulation.

This is the time of year when people say "I'm fine" and then I find three layers of tension they forgot were there. The heat masks a lot — warmth keeps muscles from seizing up completely, so things feel manageable right up until they don't. Then it's a locked-up neck on a Monday morning or a low back that goes out bending over to pick up a garden hose.

If you've been running hard all summer, now's a good time. Not when it breaks — before it breaks. The table's open Monday, Wednesday, and Friday.
