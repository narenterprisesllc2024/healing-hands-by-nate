# Five Bullet Friday — The Working Hands Edition

*August 7*

First full week of August. Hay season is wrapping up, gardens are producing faster than anyone can keep up, and your hands and forearms have been working harder than the rest of you realizes. Five things before the weekend.

---

### 1. A move — the seated figure-4 glute stretch

Your glutes are doing more work than you think right now. Every time you bend over to pick something up — a bale, a tomato, a kid — your glutes fire to bring you back up. Do that a few hundred times a day and the piriformis and glute medius lock short, which is when that deep ache in the hip starts and sometimes sends a line of pain down the back of your leg.

Sit on a chair or bench. Cross your right ankle over your left knee so your right leg makes a figure-4 shape. Sit tall — don't slouch. Lean forward from the hips, keeping your back straight, until you feel a deep stretch in the right glute. Hold 45 seconds. Switch sides.

If the stretch is too intense, just sit in the position without leaning forward — gravity and the weight of your leg are usually enough. If it's not enough, press gently on your right knee with your hand. Do this after every long bout of bending, lifting, or mowing. Two minutes total, both sides.

### 2. A tool — a massage rolling stick ($10-15)

You've got the tennis ball, the golf ball, the foam roller. The rolling stick fills the gap between them — it works on muscles you can reach with your hands but can't lie on top of.

It's a flexible stick with handles on both ends, about 18 inches long. Roll it along your forearms, your calves, or the outsides of your thighs. You control the pressure directly, so there's no guessing how much is too much. It goes where you need it, not where gravity puts it.

The reason I'm mentioning it now: after weeks of gripping — mowers, tools, hay bales, garden equipment — your forearms are probably so tight that a ball can't get the angle right. A rolling stick lets you work the full length from elbow to wrist in one pass while you're sitting on the porch. Two minutes per arm, slow passes, pause on the tender spots. There are a dozen brands; they all work. Skip the vibrating versions — your hands do fine.

### 3. A nugget — grip strength predicts more than you'd expect

Speaking of hands. There's a growing body of research linking grip strength to overall health outcomes that has nothing to do with how much you can lift.

A large-scale study published in *The Lancet* (Leong et al., 2015) followed nearly 140,000 adults across 17 countries. They found that every 5 kg decrease in grip strength was associated with a 17% increase in cardiovascular mortality and a 16% increase in all-cause mortality — independent of age, physical activity, or other risk factors. Grip strength outperformed blood pressure as a predictor of death from any cause.

The mechanism isn't fully settled, but the consensus is that grip strength is a reliable proxy for overall muscular health and systemic vitality. It's not that squeezing harder saves your life. It's that when your grip starts going, it's an early signal that the whole system is losing ground. For anyone doing manual work — farming, trades, garden work — maintaining that strength and the tissue health around it isn't vanity. It's maintenance.

### 4. A practice — the physiological sigh (90 seconds)

This one is a single breathing pattern, and it's the fastest nervous-system reset I know.

Take a deep inhale through your nose. At the top of that inhale, take one more short inhale — a second sip of air — to fully expand the lungs. Then one long, slow exhale through your mouth. That's one cycle. Do five to eight of them.

Researchers at Stanford (Balban et al., published in *Cell Reports Medicine*, 2023) tested this against box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation, and mindfulness meditation. The double-inhale, long-exhale pattern — what they call the "physiological sigh" — produced the greatest reduction in anxiety and the largest improvement in mood, and it only took five minutes. Five cycles is about 90 seconds.

The double inhale pops open the alveoli in your lungs that collapse during shallow breathing, which increases CO2 offloading on the exhale. That's the mechanism. Your body reads it as a genuine all-clear. Try it the next time you're wound up after a long day of work and can't downshift. It works faster than anything else I've recommended in this space.

### 5. A seasonal note

Early August. Hay season is winding down, but the work hasn't stopped — it's just shifted to gardens, equipment maintenance, back-to-school prep, and all the projects that got deferred while the fields needed attention. Missouri doesn't give you a quiet week in August. It just changes the flavor of the load.

If you've spent the last month working with your hands — and around here, most people have — the accumulation is in your forearms, your grip, your hips, and your low back.
