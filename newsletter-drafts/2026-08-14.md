# Five Bullet Friday — The Clenching Edition

*August 14*

Mid-August. Summer is starting to curve toward its last chapter — not done yet, but you can feel the shift coming. School's around the corner, schedules are about to change, and your body's been running the summer program for three months straight. Stress has a way of living in places you don't check. Five things this week, starting with the most overlooked one.

---

### 1. A move — the 60-second jaw release

Your jaw is probably clenched right now. Check. Teeth together, tongue pressed hard against the roof of your mouth, masseter flexed — and you didn't notice until I mentioned it. That's the pattern.

Here's a quick release. Place the pads of your fingers on your masseters — the thick muscles just in front of your ears, along your jawline. Open your mouth slightly, just enough to separate your teeth. Now make slow circles with your fingertips, moderate pressure, working from just below the cheekbone down to the corner of your jaw. Thirty seconds per side. You should feel the muscle soften under your fingers as you go.

When you're done, let your jaw hang open gently — mouth slightly open, tongue resting loosely behind your lower teeth, no contact between upper and lower teeth. That's the jaw's actual resting position. Most people haven't been there in years.

### 2. A tool — a microwaveable heat pack ($8-15)

Different from the gel ice pack from a few weeks ago — this one is for warming tissue before you work on it.

A basic microwaveable heat pack — filled with rice, flaxseed, or clay beads — is the best prep for tight jaw muscles before doing the release from bullet one. Microwave it for 60-90 seconds, drape it over the side of your face and jaw, and let it sit for five minutes. The moist heat increases blood flow to the masseter and temporalis, which makes the tissue softer and more responsive to pressure. Cold tissue resists. Warm tissue cooperates.

They work just as well on the neck, shoulders, and low back. Keep one by the bed if you clench at night — five minutes of heat on each side before sleep can reduce the overnight tension enough that you wake up without that locked-jaw, headache feeling. A rice sock made from an old tube sock works identically if you don't want to spend the money.

### 3. A nugget — your headache might start in your jaw

Something I see on the table constantly: someone books for neck pain or headaches, and when I put my hands on their jaw, it's locked solid. They had no idea.

Your masseter can generate close to 200 pounds of force on your molars — more force per unit size than almost any muscle in your body. When it's clenching all day or all night, the tension doesn't stay in the jaw. It radiates into the temporalis across your temples, into the muscles around your eyes, and down through the SCM into your neck and upper traps.

The American Academy of Orofacial Pain has documented this referral pattern in their clinical guidelines — masticatory muscle tension is one of the most common and most overlooked contributors to tension-type headaches. If you've had headaches that start at your temples or behind your eyes and nothing explains them, press into the muscle just in front of your ear. If it's tender or rock-hard, you found your starting point.

### 4. A practice — the resting jaw check-in (60 seconds, all day)

This isn't a one-time practice. It's a pattern interrupt you run throughout the day.

Set a cue — every time you pick up your phone, sit down in your car, or open your laptop. At that cue, check: are your teeth touching? They shouldn't be. Is your tongue pressed hard against the roof of your mouth? It shouldn't be. Is your jaw clenched? Release it.

The resting position: lips gently closed, teeth slightly apart — a few millimeters of daylight between upper and lower — tongue resting lightly behind your upper front teeth. No effort. This is where your jaw is supposed to live when you're not eating or talking, and almost nobody maintains it.

Each check-in takes five seconds. Do it a dozen times a day and you'll start catching the clench before it accumulates. Within a week, the resting position starts becoming the default — and that's when the headaches and neck tension ease on their own.

### 5. A seasonal note

Mid-August in Missouri. The heat hasn't broken yet, school is about to start, and the transition weeks are when stress quietly peaks. Schedules shift, routines change, sleep gets disrupted — and your body absorbs all of it, usually in the jaw, the neck, and the upper back.

This is the time of year when people realize they've been clenching for weeks. The jaw clicks. The temples throb. The neck won't turn all the way. They thought it was allergies or the heat or a bad pillow. It's usually tension that moved in gradually and never left.

If you're carrying something you've been ignoring since June, a session clears what a weekend of rest won't. The table's open Monday, Wednesday, and Friday.
